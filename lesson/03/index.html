Quick teaching steps · 30 minutes
  1. Before class: Open the PowerPoint or Canva presentation. Read the scenario and answer guidance below. If using an official video or handout, sign in and preview it first.
  2. Opening · 5 minutes: Show the title and big question. Invite made-up examples only; students may pass.
  3. Explain · 8 minutes: Use Learn It and model the safe move. If using a video, fit it into this block rather than adding time.
  4. Activity · 12 minutes: Use Try It Together, then Talk About It. Follow the script and let pairs think before showing answer guidance.
  5. Close · 5 minutes: Use Exit Check. Listen for the success criteria and record a short observation.

No projector? Read the same scenario aloud and use the tech-free Plan B below. No student devices or accounts are needed.

A message pretending to be the school

Made-up message to Jordan: “URGENT from the office. Your school account will be deleted in 10 minutes. Click http://school-prize-login.example and type your password and home address to keep your photos. Do not tell your teacher.”

Learning intentions

  • We are learning how a fake message tries to borrow trust.
  • We are learning not to type a password because a message says so.

I can statements students see

  • I can name two clues that a message may be fake.
  • I can say what to do instead of clicking or typing a password.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Names at least two clues from the made-up message, such as urgency, a strange link, a threat, or a password request.
  • Says the character should not click or type a password, and should tell a trusted adult.

Materials and prep

  • This page.
  • The message written in large print.
  • Timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Tell the class the message is invented before you read it, so it does not sound like a real school email.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. If a note said it was from me, but the writing looked wrong, what is one thing you could check before you believed it?

    Do this. Take two ideas: ask you in person, check the name, do not rush.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. Phishing is when someone pretends to be a trusted person or group to trick you into sharing personal information or a password. The message often rushes you, threatens a closed account, or offers a prize. A real school adult will not ask you to type your password into a surprise link. The safe moves are: do not click, do not type, tell a trusted adult.

    Do this. Show one honest model first: a teacher saying in the room, “Please line up.” No link. No password. Then introduce the fake message as fiction.

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. This message is made up. Underline the parts that try to rush or scare Jordan. Then decide Jordan’s next move. Do not mention real passwords, even a hint.

    Do this. Pairs underline. Build a class list of clues. Ask what Jordan should do if a friend already clicked.

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. Tell me two clues and one safe move, still about Jordan.

    Do this. Hear two students. Say that Lesson 4 turns from tricks to how our own words land.

If time is short. Underline the clues as a whole class. Skip the “friend already clicked” question.

If you have extra time. Ask how Jordan can warn a classmate without forwarding the fake link. Answer to look for: say “do not open it” in person, and tell an adult.

Made-up scenario

A message pretending to be the school

Made-up message to Jordan: “URGENT from the office. Your school account will be deleted in 10 minutes. Click http://school-prize-login.example and type your password and home address to keep your photos. Do not tell your teacher.”

  • Which parts try to rush or scare Jordan?
  • What is the sender pretending to be?
  • What should Jordan do?

Teacher answer guidance

  • Clues: URGENT, a 10-minute threat, a strange link, a password, a home address, and “do not tell your teacher.”
  • The sender is pretending to be the office. Pretending to be trusted is the trick.
  • Jordan should not click and should not type anything. Jordan should tell a teacher or a trusted adult right away.
  • If a friend already clicked, the friend should also tell an adult. Jordan should not open it “just to check.”

Quick check. What are two clues that Jordan’s message is fake, and what should Jordan do?

Look for. Any two clues from the message, plus do not click, do not type, tell a trusted adult.

Tech-free Plan B

Read the fake message twice from chart paper. Students raise a hand when they hear a clue.

Support

Number the sentences. Let a student highlight rather than explain every clue.

Extension

Rewrite the message as a real, calm notice the school might actually say, with no link and no password. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

Never ask for a real password, a guess of one, or a family email address.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

Give the three safe moves on a card: don’t click, don’t type, tell an adult. The check can be choosing the card.

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

If a child says “that happened to my uncle,” thank them and return to Jordan. Do not collect scam stories.

Assessment

Two clues plus a safe move meets the intention. One clue only is a start. Feedback names the clue they found. Reassess in Lesson 6 with a fresh example if needed.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Cross out the fake link on the board so it cannot be copied later. Shred any printouts the same day.

Sub notes

Read the “this is made up” line first. Do not click any real link a student offers.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
