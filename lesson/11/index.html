Quick teaching steps · 30 minutes
  1. Before class: Open the PowerPoint or Canva presentation. Read the scenario and answer guidance below. If using an official video or handout, sign in and preview it first.
  2. Opening · 5 minutes: Show the title and big question. Invite made-up examples only; students may pass.
  3. Explain · 8 minutes: Use Learn It and model the safe move. If using a video, fit it into this block rather than adding time.
  4. Activity · 12 minutes: Use Try It Together, then Talk About It. Follow the script and let pairs think before showing answer guidance.
  5. Close · 5 minutes: Use Exit Check. Listen for the success criteria and record a short observation.

For students

Today’s lesson

How can we tell if an online claim is credible?

We are learning

  • We are learning to pause before believing or sharing a big claim.
  • We are learning to ask who is saying it and where a more reliable source would be.

I can

  • I can spot a claim that needs checking.
  • I can name a better place to look, or a person to ask, before sharing.

What to do

  • Give one reason to pause on a huge claim.
  • Read GameFox’s post.
  • Use pause, who, better source, and exact claim.
  • Decide if Avery should share it.

The homework post

This story is made up.

Made-up post on a chat, author name “GameFox,” no other source: “Scientists say homework is now illegal in Alberta. Share this before the teachers delete it!!” Avery’s thumb is over the share button.

Check. Should Avery share the post yet? Why?

Learning intentions

  • We are learning to pause before believing or sharing a big claim.
  • We are learning to ask who is saying it and where a more reliable source would be.

I can statements students see

  • I can spot a claim that needs checking.
  • I can name a better place to look, or a person to ask, before sharing.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Says the homework claim should not be shared yet.
  • Gives a reason: the source is a nameless post, the claim is huge, or an official school or government source has not been checked.

Materials and prep

  • This page.
  • Board: Pause / Who / Better source / Exact claim.
  • Timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Tell students they will not search the internet in this lesson. Thinking is the work.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. If a post said school was cancelled forever, what is one reason to pause? No real rumours from our school, please.

    Do this. Take two reasons: big claim, unknown author, we can ask the office.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. A claim is something someone says is true. Credible means it can be trusted after we check. Our four-check is: Pause. Who is saying it? Is there a better source? What is the exact claim? A better source might be the school office, a teacher, or an official public website such as the province’s site. Common Sense teaches a method called S.I.F.T. You can open that lesson with your teacher account. Today we will use our four-check so we are not guessing their letters.

    Do this. Model a small true-looking sentence: “The gym is cold today.” Who could know? Someone who was just there. It is a small claim, easy to check.

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. Run the four-check on this made-up post. Decide if Avery should share it. Do not look it up on a phone.

    Do this. Pairs use the four words on the board. Hear one four-check aloud.

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. Should Avery share the post yet? Why or why not, in one sentence?

    Do this. Preview purpose: next time we ask what the creator wants us to do.

If time is short. Run the four-check together. Pairs only answer “share or not.”

If you have extra time. Change the post so it might be credible: “The office notice on the classroom door says early dismissal Friday.” Ask what Avery should still do. Look for: read the notice, ask the teacher.

Made-up scenario

The homework post

Made-up post on a chat, author name “GameFox,” no other source: “Scientists say homework is now illegal in Alberta. Share this before the teachers delete it!!” Avery’s thumb is over the share button.

  • What is the exact claim?
  • Who is saying it?
  • Is there a better source?
  • Should Avery share it yet?

Teacher answer guidance

  • The claim is that homework is now illegal in Alberta. That is huge and specific.
  • The only speaker is a nameless chat user. No scientist, school, or government source is shown.
  • A better check would be a teacher, the school office, or an official government or school notice. Friends resharing is not a better source.
  • Avery should not share it yet. Pause is the correct move.

Quick check. Should Avery share the post yet? Why?

Look for. No. The source is unknown and the claim is large. Avery should ask a teacher or check an official notice.

Tech-free Plan B

The post lives on chart paper. Students tap the unknown author and the huge claim.

Support

Leave the four words visible. Accept “ask the teacher” as the better source.

Extension

Underline the exact claim and separate it from the excited emoji language. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

Do not repeat live rumours about the school, students, or families.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

Read the post aloud twice. A student may answer only “share or wait.”

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

If someone pulls out a phone, thank them and say the check is on paper today.

Assessment

“Do not share yet” plus a reason meets the intention. Sharing because “it would be funny” does not. Feedback: “You paused. Add who would be a better source.” Reassess with the extra-time example or in Lesson 12’s purpose talk.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Cross out the fake post so it is not copied into a notebook as a fact.

Sub notes

Do not tell students the claim is a real law. It is fiction for practice. If they ask, say you would check an official source before believing it.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
