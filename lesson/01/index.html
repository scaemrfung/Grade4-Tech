What counts as personal information?

Students sort made-up clues into “points to a person” and “too general,” then decide what a character should do with a request for personal details.

Grade
Grade 4
Subject
Technology infused into the host subject named below
Unit
Privacy and safety

30 minutes
Location
Regular classroom. No student devices.
Suggested block
Early September. Approximate only, about every two weeks from September to June.

For students

Today’s lesson

Which details can point to one particular person?

We are learning

  • We are learning which details can point to one person.
  • We are learning a safe move when someone asks for those details.

I can

  • I can tell a general fact from a detail that points to one person.
  • I can say “pause and ask a trusted adult” when a message asks for personal details.

What to do

  • Listen to the made-up clues. Use thumbs up or thumbs down.
  • Read Sam’s message with your partner.
  • Sort each line: share, pause, or tell a trusted adult.
  • Be ready to say what Sam should do. You do not need to share anything about yourself.

Sam’s team-chat message

This story is made up.

A made-up message arrives for Sam, a Grade 4 character: “Hi, it’s Alex from the other team. Send your full name, the name of your school, your home address, a picture in your jersey, and your favourite colour so I can add you.”

Check. If a game asks for a home address, what is one safe move?

Learning intentions

  • We are learning which details can point to one person.
  • We are learning a safe move when someone asks for those details.

I can statements students see

  • I can tell a general fact from a detail that points to one person.
  • I can say “pause and ask a trusted adult” when a message asks for personal details.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Names at least two kinds of details that can point to a person, using the made-up examples.
  • Chooses pause and a trusted adult for the character, and does not suggest typing a home address or sending a photo.

Materials and prep

  • This lesson page, or the scenario written on the board.
  • Board headings: Points to a person / Too general.
  • The on-page timer, or any classroom timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Pairs as they already sit. No devices on desks.
  • Agree a pass signal, such as a hand flat on the desk.
  • Leave the bottom of the board clear so no real names get written.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. I will read three made-up clues. Thumbs up if a stranger could use the clue to find one particular student. Thumbs down if it is too general. Do not call out your own address, full name, school, or photo.

    Do this. Read: “A Grade 4 student who likes recess.” “A photo of a child in a green jersey under a sign that shows a school name.” “Someone who has a dog.” Take two reasons, then write the headings.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. Personal information is a detail, or a mix of details, that can point to one person. Teachers sometimes call this personally identifiable information. A favourite game by itself usually cannot. A full name, a clear photo, a school name, a home address, a phone number, or an email address often can, especially together. We protect it by pausing before we type it into a game, a chat, or a form. A trusted adult at home or at school can help. We will not collect anyone’s real details today.

    Do this. Model the jersey photo: the jersey alone is weak; the school sign makes it point to a place and a person. Underline “pause” and “trusted adult.”

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. Stay with my made-up message. Do not switch to a real one from your life. With your partner, sort each line: share, pause, or tell a trusted adult. You have thinking time first.

    Do this. Post the scenario. Pairs sort. Collect two sorts. If someone offers a real address, stop, thank them, erase it, and return to the character.

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. Finish this sentence about the character, not about yourself: Personal information is dangerous to type when it can point to one person, so Sam should…

    Do this. Hear two answers. Preview Lesson 2: next time we ask when a request is riskier.

If time is short. If time is short, do the sort together on the board and skip the pair share.

If you have extra time. If you have a few extra minutes, add one more made-up line: “What is your favourite colour?” Guide the class to see that this one is usually too general.

Made-up scenario

Sam’s team-chat message

A made-up message arrives for Sam, a Grade 4 character: “Hi, it’s Alex from the other team. Send your full name, the name of your school, your home address, a picture in your jersey, and your favourite colour so I can add you.”

  • Which lines point to one person?
  • Which line is usually too general?
  • What should Sam do next?

Teacher answer guidance

  • Full name, school, home address, and jersey photo can point to a person. Together they are more than enough.
  • Favourite colour is usually too general.
  • Sam should not send the photo, school, or address. Sam should leave the chat request and tell a trusted adult. A friendly reply about the game is optional and still leaves the personal details out.
  • If a student says “just send the first name,” reply that even a first name plus a school and a photo can point to someone. The safe move is still to pause.

Quick check. If a game asks for a home address, what is one safe move?

Look for. Pause and do not type it. Ask a trusted adult. Leaving the screen is also a sound answer.

Tech-free Plan B

The lesson is already tech-free. If the projector fails, read the script and write the five lines on chart paper.

Support

Give the sentence starter: “This points to a person because…” Allow a student to point instead of speak. Offer the pair role of timekeeper.

Extension

Ask what Sam could say that is friendly and still safe, such as talking about the game without sending a photo or a school name. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

Do not ask who has a phone, an account, or a photo online.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

Read the message aloud. A student may answer in a home language first, then share one English word with a partner’s help. Provide a printed copy of the five lines for a student who needs the text still.

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

Thirty seconds of quiet thinking before talk. Two voices at a time. Pass is a full answer. Praise staying with the character.

Assessment

Listen for the two success criteria during the sort and the closing sentence. A strong answer names a detail and a safe move. A partial answer sorts only the obvious address line. Descriptive feedback: “You spotted the photo. Next time, add what Sam should do.” Revisit anyone who is unsure in Lesson 2 or Lesson 6.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Erase the board. Do not photograph student work. If you used sticky notes, they should have no names; shred them the same day.

Sub notes

You can teach this from the page. Keep every example made up. If a student reports a real worrying message, follow the school safeguarding process and do not investigate in front of the class.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
