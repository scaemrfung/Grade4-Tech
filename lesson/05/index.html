A fair group chat

Students look at a made-up group chat and choose inclusive moves that do not spread the hurt further.

Subject

For students

Today’s lesson

How can we be a good friend when a group leaves someone out?

We are learning

  • We are learning that a group chat can leave someone out even when nobody says a mean sentence.
  • We are learning moves that include the person and do not spread a screenshot.

I can

  • I can say how the left-out character might feel.
  • I can choose a move that includes them or gets adult help.

What to do

  • Answer the thumbs question. A real chat is not required.
  • Read Priya’s story.
  • Choose a helpful move and name a move that would make it worse.

The game plan without Priya

This story is made up.

Sam, Ari, and Jules are in a made-up group chat planning a weekend floor-hockey game. Priya, who usually plays, is not in the chat. Ari writes, “Don’t add Priya. It is funnier with just us.” Sam feels uneasy and has a screenshot.

Check. What is one move that helps Priya, and one move that spreads the hurt?

Learning intentions

  • We are learning that a group chat can leave someone out even when nobody says a mean sentence.
  • We are learning moves that include the person and do not spread a screenshot.

I can statements students see

  • I can say how the left-out character might feel.
  • I can choose a move that includes them or gets adult help.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Names the harm as being left out, not as “just a small chat.”
  • Chooses at least one fair move: add them, check in at school, or tell a trusted adult if it keeps happening. Does not recommend forwarding a screenshot to more people.

Materials and prep

  • This page.
  • Board: Include / Check in / Get help.
  • Timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Groups of three if the room allows, otherwise pairs. Roles can be rotated.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. Is it possible to hurt someone in a group chat without using a mean word? Thumbs up, down, or sideways. We will use a made-up story, not a real chat.

    Do this. Take two ideas. Accept “leaving someone out” if it comes up; if not, teach it next.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. A group chat feels public and fast. Leaving one friend off the plan can hurt more because several people saw it. A good friend notices. You can add the person, check in face to face, and refuse to pass on a screenshot. If it happens again, or if you feel unsure, tell a trusted adult. You do not have to solve it alone.

    Do this. Write the three moves. Model a calm check-in: “We planned the game without you. Want to be in the next one?”

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. Read Priya’s story. In your group, say how Priya might feel, what Sam could do today, and what would make it worse. Do not describe a real group you are in.

    Do this. Listen to two groups. Stop any suggestion to post the screenshot “so everyone sees.”

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. What is one move that helps Priya, and one move that would spread the hurt?

    Do this. Close by naming Lesson 6 as a review if the group starts pushing someone to join in.

If time is short. Skip group talk and do the three moves as a class on one story.

If you have extra time. Ask what Sam should do if Priya says “I am fine” but still looks left out. Look for: respect the answer, leave the door open, and still not spread the chat.

Made-up scenario

The game plan without Priya

Sam, Ari, and Jules are in a made-up group chat planning a weekend floor-hockey game. Priya, who usually plays, is not in the chat. Ari writes, “Don’t add Priya. It is funnier with just us.” Sam feels uneasy and has a screenshot.

  • How might Priya feel if this continues?
  • What can Sam do today?
  • What would make it worse?

Teacher answer guidance

  • Priya may feel confused, left out, or embarrassed. The harm is exclusion, even without a name-calling word.
  • Sam can say in the chat “I am adding Priya,” check in with Priya at school, and refuse to forward the screenshot. If Ari pushes again, Sam tells a trusted adult.
  • Worse moves: forwarding the screenshot, laughing, or telling Priya “everyone thinks you are annoying.”

Quick check. What is one move that helps Priya, and one move that spreads the hurt?

Look for. Help: add Priya, check in, or tell an adult. Spread: forward the screenshot or gossip about it.

Tech-free Plan B

Three students may hold role cards: Priya, Sam, and the chat. They read only the printed lines.

Support

Sentence starters: “Priya might feel…” “Sam could…” “A worse move would be…”

Extension

Write the exact words Sam could say at recess, in one or two sentences. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

No real group names, no screenshots from home, no ranking of classmates.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

A student who finds social talk hard can be the move-chooser from a list of three rather than inventing wording.

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

If the class points at a real person, interrupt: “We are on Priya, who is made up.” Reset seats if needed.

Assessment

Including or getting help meets the intention. Laughing it off as nothing does not. Feedback: “You noticed Priya was missing. Add what Sam does next.” Reassess in Lesson 6.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Collect role cards. Do not leave a student’s real name on a role card.

Sub notes

Keep the story fictional. If a student discloses ongoing exclusion, follow school safeguarding and offer a private check-in later, not a class discussion.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
