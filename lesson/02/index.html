When is sharing a bigger risk?

For students

Today’s lesson

When should we be more careful about personal details?

We are learning

  • We are learning that the same detail can be a smaller or bigger risk depending on who asks and why.

I can

  • I can ask who, why, and what could go wrong before a character shares a personal detail.

What to do

  • Think about the opening question. You may pass.
  • Read Request A and Request B.
  • Answer who, why, and what could go wrong.
  • Choose which request Riley should refuse.

Two requests for Riley

This story is made up.

Request A: The teacher says, “I already have the class list. Please just open your book.” Request B: A pop-up in a game says, “You won a tablet. Type your full name, home address, and password in the next 60 seconds.”

Check. Which request should Riley refuse, and what is one warning sign?

Learning intentions

  • We are learning that the same detail can be a smaller or bigger risk depending on who asks and why.

I can statements students see

  • I can ask who, why, and what could go wrong before a character shares a personal detail.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Uses who and why to explain why the stranger’s prize is riskier than the teacher’s class list.
  • Names one warning sign, such as a prize, a rush, or a person the character does not know.

Materials and prep

  • This page.
  • Board: Who? Why? What could go wrong?
  • Timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Same pairs. No devices. Recap Lesson 1 in one sentence before you start.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. Last time we said some details point to one person. Thumbs in the middle if you are not sure yet. Is it always unsafe to tell your teacher your name?

    Do this. Take two answers. Land on: a name at school, for attendance, is a different situation from typing it for a stranger.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. Risk is the chance that sharing could cause a problem. Ask three questions. Who is asking? Why do they need it? What could go wrong if the detail travels? A teacher taking attendance already knows the class. A stranger offering a prize does not need a home address. Warning signs include a rush, a prize, a secret, or a request to leave the app and talk somewhere else.

    Do this. Model the teacher list as lower risk, and still say we do not add extra details the teacher did not ask for.

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. Here are two made-up requests for the character Riley. Do not use your own logins. With a partner, answer who, why, and what could go wrong for each one.

    Do this. Give thinking time. Hear one pair for request A and one pair for request B. Correct the idea that a prize makes a request safe.

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. Which request should Riley refuse, and what is one warning sign?

    Do this. Collect two answers. Preview phishing: next time the message will pretend to be someone Riley trusts.

If time is short. Model request A together and let pairs do only request B.

If you have extra time. Ask what Riley could do if a classmate, not a stranger, asks for a home address for a party invitation. Guide them to: tell a parent or caregiver, do not post the address in a group chat.

Made-up scenario

Two requests for Riley

Request A: The teacher says, “I already have the class list. Please just open your book.” Request B: A pop-up in a game says, “You won a tablet. Type your full name, home address, and password in the next 60 seconds.”

  • Who is asking in each request?
  • Why would they need the detail?
  • What could go wrong?

Teacher answer guidance

  • Request A is the teacher, for learning, and no extra personal detail is required. Risk is lower. Riley still should not add a home address.
  • Request B is an unknown pop-up. A prize and a countdown are warning signs. A password should not be typed. Riley should close it and tell a trusted adult.
  • If a student says the tablet might be real, reply: a real prize would not need a password from a child in a hurry. Tell an adult and leave it.

Quick check. Which request should Riley refuse, and what is one warning sign?

Look for. Request B. Warning signs include the prize, the countdown, the stranger, the address, or the password.

Tech-free Plan B

Write both requests on the board if you cannot project the page.

Support

Leave the three questions on the board. Offer a partner who reads. Accept a one-word warning sign.

Extension

Invent one sentence Riley can send that refuses the prize without being rude. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

Do not collect real account names or ask who has talked to someone they do not know.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

Point to Who, Why, and What as you ask. Let students hold up 1, 2, or 3 fingers for low, medium, or high risk before they explain.

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

Stop any real story with: “Thank you. We are staying with Riley so nobody has to share a real event.”

Assessment

Look for who, why, and a warning sign. Feedback: “You named the prize. Add who is asking.” Students who mix up the two requests can show the sort again in Lesson 3 or Lesson 6.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Clear the board of anything that is not the made-up example.

Sub notes

Teach both requests from the page. Do not ask students to log in or to prove they have an account.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
