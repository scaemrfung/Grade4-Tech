Quick teaching steps · 30 minutes

  2. Opening · 5 minutes: Show the title and big question. Invite made-up examples only; students may pass.
  3. Explain · 8 minutes: Use Learn It and model the safe move. If using a video, fit it into this block rather than adding time.
  4. Activity · 12 minutes: Use Try It Together, then Talk About It. Follow the script and let pairs think before showing answer guidance.

For students

Today’s lesson

Who influences us online, and are they positive role models?

We are learning

  • We are learning that popularity is not the same as being a positive role model.
  • We are learning questions that test a made-up creator.

I can

  • I can say what Coach Kay wants the audience to do.
  • I can give a reason for or against calling Coach Kay a positive role model.

What to do

  • Answer the popularity question using a book character or a pass.
  • Listen to Coach Kay’s clip.
  • Test truth, kindness, and selling.

Coach Kay’s clip

This story is made up.

Made-up creator Coach Kay always shows the best goal, never a miss, and says: “Buy these shoes and you will score like me. If you cannot, you are not trying.” The comments are full of people saying they want the shoes.

Check. What is one reason Coach Kay is not automatically a positive role model?

Curriculum outcomes

Host subject

Physical Education and Wellness, Grade 4

Organizing idea. “Character Development: Exploration of life opportunities and virtues develops resilience and personal talents and promotes lifelong learning.”

Guiding question. “How can a variety of life experiences influence resilience and perseverance?”

Learning outcome. “Students interpret how resilience and perseverance can be influenced by a variety of life experiences.”

Knowledge. “Strategies that support resilience include identifying a purpose, seeking positive role models, focusing on the solution instead of the challenge, breaking down tasks into smaller, achievable goals.”

Understanding. “Resilience may result in the increased ability to respond to future adversity. Resilience is supported by development of perseverance over time.”

Skills and procedures. “Describe strategies that support resilience.”

The outcome is about resilience and life experiences. It does not name influencers. This lesson works toward “seeking positive role models” by testing a made-up online creator.

Learning intentions

  • We are learning that popularity is not the same as being a positive role model.
  • We are learning questions that test a made-up creator.

I can statements students see

  • I can say what Coach Kay wants the audience to do.
  • I can give a reason for or against calling Coach Kay a positive role model.

Success criteria / evidence of learning

  • Says Coach Kay is trying to persuade people to buy shoes and to believe the shoes cause skill.
  • Uses at least one test: truth about what is hard, kindness, or whether a sale is the real goal.

Materials and prep

  • This page.
  • Board: Truth / Kindness / Selling.
  • Timer.

No Chromebooks, no student logins, and no device project. A projector is optional. The page is the lesson.

Room or space setup

  • Agree not to name real influencers, so nobody’s taste is ranked.

Lesson sequence (timed)

The four parts total 30 minutes: 5 + 8 + 12 + 5.

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    Say this. Can a person be popular and still be a poor role model? Think of a character from a book if you want. No real online names.

    Do this. Take two reasons. Connect to “seeking positive role models” as a resilience strategy, in student-friendly words: we copy people on purpose.

  2. Explain and model · 8 min

    Say this. An influencer is a person who tries to shape what others do or buy. Influence is not automatically bad. A positive role model tells the truth about effort, treats people kindly, and is honest when they are selling. If the message says “buy this and you will be great,” the purpose is to persuade. We can enjoy a clip and still refuse the lesson it teaches.

    Do this. Model a librarian who posts “Reading got easier when I practised ten minutes a day.” Truth about effort: yes. Selling: no. Kinder example of influence.

  3. Guided scenario · 12 min

    Say this. Test Coach Kay with truth, kindness, and selling. Decide if you would want a younger student to copy Coach Kay. Stay with Kay, who is made up.

    Do this. Pairs decide and give a reason. Hear both a yes-with-limits and a no, if they appear. The no is better supported.

  4. Check and close · 5 min

    Say this. What is one reason Coach Kay is not automatically a positive role model?

    Do this. Preview digital footprints: what we leave behind, including what we copy and share.

If time is short. Use the librarian model and do Coach Kay as a whole-class test.

If you have extra time. Ask what Kay would need to change to be a better role model. Look for: show the missed shots, skip the false promise, be honest that the shoes are an ad.

Made-up scenario

Coach Kay’s clip

Made-up creator Coach Kay always shows the best goal, never a miss, and says: “Buy these shoes and you will score like me. If you cannot, you are not trying.” The comments are full of people saying they want the shoes.

  • What does Kay want viewers to do?
  • Does Kay tell the truth about what is hard?
  • Is Kay a positive role model? Why?

Teacher answer guidance

  • Kay wants viewers to buy shoes and to believe the shoes create skill. Main purpose: persuade.
  • Kay does not tell the truth about effort, because only the best goal is shown, and the insult says struggle means you are not trying.
  • Kay is popular in the story and is not a positive role model on this clip. A better role model would show practice, speak kindly, and label an ad as an ad.

Quick check. What is one reason Coach Kay is not automatically a positive role model?

Look for. The clip hides the hard parts, insults people who struggle, or pretends shoes replace practice.

Tech-free Plan B

Read Kay’s script like a radio ad. Students raise a hand on the selling sentence.

Support

The three test words stay on the board. Students can answer yes or no to each.

Extension

Write one sentence a positive sports role model might post instead. The extension stays inside the block. It is not homework and not a project.

Safety notes

Physical activity: not applicable. There is no gym task and no equipment to set out.

If a student names a real creator, say “we are not discussing real people today” and move on. Do not shame the student.

Do not ask students for real names, addresses, schools, photos, usernames, passwords, screen-time diaries, or upsetting personal experiences. If a student discloses harm, follow the school’s safeguarding process. Do not investigate in front of the class.

Differentiation and inclusive supports

Provide the librarian and Kay side by side so the contrast is visible.

Follow ISP supports listed in the class binder. Do not write medical details or student names into this public site or onto a slide you will publish.

Classroom management cues

No impressions of real creators. No “who do you follow?”

Assessment

A reason tied to truth or selling meets the intention. “Kay is cool” does not. Feedback: “You noticed the shoes. Say whether the promise is truthful.” Students can reassess with the extension sentence.

Use observations, conversations, and products linked to the success criteria. Feedback describes what you noticed and one next step. There is no numeric or percent grade. If you use Elk Island Public Schools’ Grades 3–6 descriptive levels, the words are Not Yet Meeting Expectations, Minimally Meeting Expectations, Solidifying Expectations, and Mastering Expectations. A student can show the skill again in a flex lesson or in a one-minute conversation. Do not file personal stories.

Exit and clean-up

Erase any real name that landed on the board.

Sub notes

Do not let the class vote on real people. Coach Kay is the whole discussion.

The say-this lines are the lesson. Keep the 5, 8, 12, and 5 timing. Skip the extension if the clock is tight.
